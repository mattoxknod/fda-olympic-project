120 Years of Olympic Data

Mattox Knod · Financial Data Analytics

This report explores 271,116 athlete-event records spanning every Olympic Games from Athens 1896 to Rio 2016, Summer and Winter combined. It looks at which countries have dominated the medal table and how that has shifted over time, how physically different Olympic sports are from one another, and how the Games went from an almost entirely male event to one approaching gender parity.

Finding 1 of 8

The United States has won nearly 2.5 times as many medals as any other country

Counting one medal per country per event (so a team gold counts once, not once per athlete on the roster), the United States has won 2,823 medals across 120 years of competition — well ahead of the Soviet Union (1,197) and Germany (1,004). Great Britain, France, and Italy round out the top five. Fifteen countries account for the vast majority of medals ever awarded, out of 230 that have won at least one. Click a bar to explore that country on the dashboard.

Finding 2 of 8

The medal leaderboard has had two eras: a US-Soviet rivalry, then US dominance

Tracking the six most decorated countries year by year shows the Soviet Union running neck-and-neck with, and at some Games surpassing, the United States through the Cold War era. After the Soviet Union's dissolution in 1991, the United States pulled well clear of the field, while Germany, Great Britain, France, and Italy have traded places well behind the top two.

Finding 3 of 8

Winter medals are far more concentrated among a handful of countries than Summer medals

147 countries have won at least one Summer medal, and the top 10 of them hold 54% of all Summer medals ever awarded. Winter sport tells a very different story: only 46 countries have ever won a Winter medal at all, and the top 10 hold 69% of them. Winter sport's reliance on snow, ice, and cold-weather infrastructure keeps the field of contenders much smaller.

Finding 4 of 8

Host countries win more medals in their own Games — every time, in this data

Comparing eight modern Summer host countries' medal count in their host year against their own average in every other Games they appear in, all eight won more medals at home. The swing ranges from a modest +12% for the United States in 1996 (already a huge program) to Australia more than tripling its typical haul in Sydney 2000 (58 medals vs. an average of 17). Across all eight cases the average increase is +141%.

Finding 5 of 8

The average Olympian's age has barely moved in 120 years

Despite huge changes in training, sports science, and the professionalization of the Games, the average competitor's age has stayed remarkably stable — 23.1 years old at the first modern Games in 1896, and 26.4 in 2016, with the average sitting in the mid-20s for almost every Games in between.

Finding 6 of 8

Sport-specific body types are dramatic: basketball players average 31cm taller than gymnasts

Looking at Summer Games from 2000-2016, basketball players average 191.8cm and 87.2kg, with volleyball close behind. At the other end, gymnasts average just 160.6cm and 54.6kg — the shortest, lightest athletes of any sport in the data. Weightlifting stands out as an exception to the general "taller means heavier" pattern: weightlifters are almost as short as gymnasts (166.7cm) but carry far more mass (79.2kg), reflecting a sport built around strength-to-weight rather than height.

Finding 7 of 8

Women went from 0% of Olympians in 1896 to 45% in 2016

No women competed at the first modern Olympics in 1896. Participation crept up slowly for decades, then accelerated after the 1970s. By the 2012 London Games women made up 44.3% of competitors, and by Rio 2016 that reached 45.0% — the closest the Games have ever come to gender parity.

Finding 8 of 8

Boxing and wrestling were the last traditionally male sports to add women's events

Wrestling (competed since 1896) didn't add women's events until 2004; boxing (since 1904) waited until 2012, the most recent addition of any sport that had been part of the Games for decades. In the 2016 field, boxing remained the most male-skewed sport (12.7% female), followed by wrestling (32.4%) and canoeing (33.9%). At the other extreme, synchronized swimming and rhythmic gymnastics remain women-only events, and volleyball, fencing, and taekwondo were within a point or two of an even split.

    About this data

    • Source. "120 years of Olympic history: athletes and results," originally compiled by Randi H. Griffin from sports-reference.com and published on Kaggle, downloaded here via its public Zenodo mirror.
    • One row is one athlete's participation in one event at one Olympic Games (Summer or Winter), 1896-2016. 271,116 rows, 15 columns.
    • Rows dropped. None outright. Rows missing age, height, weight, or medal are excluded only from the specific calculation that needs that field.
    • Medal counts are de-duplicated. Because every athlete on a team gets their own row, a single team gold (e.g. a 12-player roster) would count as 12 medals if rows were summed directly. Every medal count in this report counts one medal per (Games, Event, Country, Medal color), so a team medal counts once for that country.
    • Athlete counts are de-duplicated per Games. An athlete competing in several events at the same Games has one row per event; participation counts and the age and gender-share figures count each athlete once per Games.
    • Host-country bump uses eight modern Summer Games with a single, unambiguous host nation (Seoul 1988 through Rio 2016), excluding Games hosted by since-split or since-renamed nations. "Host year" medals are compared to that country's average across all its other Summer Games appearances in the data.
    • Height and weight by sport uses Summer Games from 2000-2016 only, and drops any sport with fewer than 30 athlete-Games in that window.
    • The globe can be dragged to rotate and scrolled to zoom. It colors each country by its all-time de-duplicated medal count (log-scaled, since the United States so heavily outweighs most countries) and shows unique athletes on hover. Host-city markers rank that Games' "top 3" by total medal count, the same method used everywhere else in this report — not the gold-first tiebreak the IOC's official medal table uses, which can rank countries differently. Country coverage on the globe is not exhaustive: small territories and historical entities (e.g. the Soviet Union) without a matching present-day land boundary are simply left uncolored rather than guessed at.
    • Country groupings use the NOC column, not Team, since Team includes individual crew/club names (e.g. multi-boat sailing entries) while NOC is the standardized country code used throughout the dataset.
    • Every number on this page is reproducible by running node scripts/analyze.js, which reads data/athlete_events.csv from scratch and regenerates scripts/findings.json. Full detail on every filter and field is in the repository's README.
